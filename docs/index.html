MagSID Editor v1.63

Release date: 2026-10-25

A Windows tracker for Commodore 64 music. Write in a classic tracker grid or a DAW-style piano roll, hear it through reSID, and export a .sid that plays exactly the same on the real machine.

Download for Windows Free and open source · Windows 10/11, 64-bit · needs the .NET 8 Desktop Runtime

Preview 0.6 Also for macOS · Apple Silicon macOS · Intel Linux · x64 release notes opening it on a Mac

What it does

Two views of the same song, a player that fits in a few hundred bytes, and nothing else to install.

🎹

Retro and DAW

A classic tracker grid for typing fast, and an arrangement with a piano roll for seeing the whole song - drag clips between channels, split, glue and erase them. Both edit the same song at once.

⏺️

Record from MIDI

Play into the piano roll from a MIDI keyboard, with a one-bar pre-roll, an optional click, and punch-in between the locators. Hold a chord to make an arpeggio.

💾

Tiny player

The C64 player is written in 6502 assembly and only carries the effects a song uses. A finished song, player included, is around 750-1250 bytes - and in the editor it plays through reSID, on a 6581 or an 8580, as the C64 will.

🎯

Export to .SID file

At any load address. The editor builds the file itself, byte for byte what the assembler would make - no assembler or emulator needed.

📼

Built-in SID player

Open any .sid to play it, with a spectrum and a scope per voice, and save it as WAV, MP3 or FLAC.

🎼

Import MIDI

Turn a .mid file into a song the C64 way: bass and drums on channel 1, the chords read bar by bar as arpeggios, the lead on top - and each General MIDI sound mapped to an instrument from the demo songs, or one picked from the sound bank.

💾

Import SID

Open any .sid tune as a song: MagSID plays it, writes down what it does to the SID chip and rebuilds it as notes, patterns and up to 16 instruments - snare, bass and lead first. It tells you how much of the tune the song sounds like, and picks the row speed - funk speed for a tune that swings - that sounds most in the fewest bytes.

🎛️

Sound bank

25 sounds from the demo songs - instruments, drums, and arpeggios at three speeds. Click a card to hear it, double-click to add it. Save any instrument as a small JSON file to build up your own.

Small by design

Code for an effect is only assembled when a song uses it.

Get started

  1. Install the .NET 8 Desktop Runtime for x64, if Windows doesn't already have it.
  2. Download the zip from the latest release, unpack it anywhere and run MagSID-Editor.exe. There is no installer.
  3. Open a song from the example songs with Menu → Open (F10). F1 plays from the top, F2 from the cursor, F4 stops.
  4. Menu → About lists every key and control.
The exe is not code-signed, so Windows SmartScreen may say it comes from an unknown publisher. Choose More info → Run anyway.
macOS and Linux get the cross-platform preview, with .NET built in - nothing else to install. It has everything the Windows editor has - Import MIDI and SID, Optimize, Signal Path, Digi to SID-osc, MIDI keyboards. On Linux, unpack it and run ./MagSID.

On a Mac, unzip the download and move MagSID to Applications. The app is not notarised by Apple yet, so macOS stops it the first time it is opened:

  • macOS 15 Sequoia and later: try to open MagSID once and close the warning. Then go to System Settings → Privacy & Security, scroll down to the line about MagSID and choose Open Anyway.
  • macOS 14 and earlier: right-click MagSID, choose Open, then Open again.
  • If macOS says MagSID is damaged and can't be opened, it is the mark macOS puts on everything downloaded from the internet, not the app. Remove it in Terminal:
xattr -dr com.apple.quarantine /Applications/MagSID.app

That tells macOS to stop checking this one app, so only do it for software you trust. Either way it is needed once, not every time.

The story behind MagSID

From a Turbo Assembler player in 1990 to the editor released at Mysdata 2026.

Where it started

I started developing MagSID back in 1990 in Turbo Assembler. I quickly found out that I needed an editor for it, so I started on a simple editor in BASIC that mainly manipulated the music player code in memory.

Years passed, and I took a long break from the C64 until I returned in 2010. By then everyone told me GoatTracker was the standard for composing music for demos, so I didn't take MagSID any further for a while. But I soon learned that when coders really want to break the limits of the C64, the music often has to be tailor-made for specific demo parts. This became very obvious when we in Censor Design were working on the demo Serpent in 2014. That was the first time I revisited my old MagSID code, and I decided to create a new small music player for demo parts and games that needed either a CPU-efficient player or a player that fits in very little memory.

Tiny songs

Around 2018-2019 I got more and more requests to make music for very tight memory spaces. I focused on improving the MagSID player to use as small a memory footprint as possible, and released songs for many 4 KB games and intros around that time. I have also made special versions of MagSID that support more complex sound-effect features in games made by Megastyle.

Over the years I moved between several assemblers - Turbo Assembler, Kick Assembler - but finally settled on the improved version of C6510 released by Krikkit / Censor Design, originally made by CRT.

Wanting something more visual

I kept working in the assembler sources and making it easier to edit my songs directly in them, but in the end I found it very limiting. One of my goals had long been a more visual way of making music, like I'm used to from modern DAWs such as Cubase, Cakewalk, Logic Pro and Ableton - with instruments that look and work more like Omnisphere and the Native Instruments Komplete VSTs.

I was also thinking of Multistyle Labs' famous C64 songs, with their great instrumentation and sounds. I was never that technical myself when it came to understanding how many of those sounds were made, so I started to imagine a more visual approach, where you could see the pulse width change rather than writing the automation as hex values in a pulse table, as in GoatTracker.

That idea took shape, and during and after the pandemic, around 2022-2023, I worked on a C#/.NET WinForms editor for MagSID. But I found it very hard to find the time to develop it the way I wanted, and slowly the project died.

A fresh start

At the start of August 2026, Slaygon / Censor Design posted a preview of his new editor, SIDBase. Around the same time Bob kept nagging me to try Claude Code, and I just felt I had to try to make something myself. My old WinForms experiment wasn't working the way I wanted, and I was still mostly writing songs in the assembler sources whenever people asked for new MagSID tunes.

I was very happy with my MagSID player code in assembler, so what I really needed was an editor. I started from scratch, with my old ideas in mind, and built it together with Claude Code.

Mysdata and the release

By the beginning of September I had a new editor that felt stable and worked quite well. When I told the Mysdata organisers about it, we decided that one of the talks at Mysdata would be about the MagSID editor. At that point I was still mainly making an editor for my own use, and just playing around with it. But during Mysdata different people also asked me to release it, so I decided on the spot to do that, as an outside-party release.

Thanks to Slaygon and SIDBase

It wasn't finished, and it was also clearly inspired by many of the ideas Slaygon had worked on for SIDBase. I never intentionally copied Slaygon's designs - my own work was inspired by Cubase and VSTs in particular. But we both used AI in our development, and we come from the same background of working with DAWs, so looking back at the MagSID editor there are many things that are very similar, even though I never intended them to be. I vibe-coded my editor the way I wanted it to work, as the workflow I needed to compose in.

With that said, I want to give a big thank you to Slaygon for ideas and inspiration, and I also want to promote his editor - it is far more complex than MagSID. The goal of MagSID was always small-footprint music, SFX-driven games, and small rasterline-friendly players for specific demo parts. It was never meant to be a full-blown player and editor with all the advanced automation and instrumentation of GoatTracker and similar editors.

What's next

Since the release, MagSID has received a lot of new requests, such as MIDI song import and sound banks. I have also started on a Digi-2-SID feature, similar to using noise plus ring modulation and sync for speech synthesis in 1x-speed songs. Not everything is done yet - maybe I'll aim to finish things off in future releases - but as of now I am quite happy with how MagSID turned out.

I am sorry if this release of MagSID upset anyone. My intention was always the opposite when, after my talk at Mysdata, I settled on a public release: sharing is caring, and I hope MagSID can be a useful, working tool for others who want to make small-footprint songs for future 4 KB productions.
